Closing the loop: why a model gets better only if its lab data comes back

A model does not get better simply because it makes predictions. It gets better when reality answers back.

Authors · date · reading time: not supplied

Contents

Summary

AI models can generate candidate molecules, proteins, and experimental designs faster than laboratories can test them. But a prediction is not experimental evidence. Its value becomes clearer when laboratory measurements return to the model and influence what happens next.

In a 2023 catalyst-discovery study, researchers incorporated measured stereoselectivity values into the model’s dataset after each experimental round. The mean absolute prediction error fell from 0.36 in round one to 0.10 in round three, while later rounds identified more high-performing catalysts [1]. A separate self-driving laboratory study demonstrated how experimental characterization can update a Bayesian optimizer’s next-experiment recommendations [2].

The lesson is not simply that AI needs more data. It needs reliable, structured experimental feedback linked to the original designs, including measured outcomes, failures, and relevant experimental context. Active learning can then help prioritize which candidates to test next.

However, feedback does not guarantee improvement. Results depend on data quality, experimental selection, the objective being optimized, and how well the new measurements represent the intended discovery problem. For AI-driven discovery, the goal is therefore to build a repeatable cycle in which each experiment informs the next decision. Benchmarking Bayesian optimization across materials domains: [15].

01Closing the loop

In a 2023 study, researchers used Bayesian optimization to identify aluminum-based catalysts for controlling the stereochemistry of a polymerization reaction. The model proposed candidates, chemists synthesized and tested 33 catalysts, and the measured results were added to the training data to refine predictions for the next round [1].

The improvement was measurable: the mean absolute error between predicted and experimentally measured stereoselectivity fell from 0.36 in the first round to 0.10 in the third. Later rounds also identified a greater proportion of high-performing catalysts. This illustrates the central idea of closed-loop learning: predict, experiment, capture the results, update the model, and choose what to test next.

For AI-driven drug discovery and protein engineering, the challenge is not just generating more candidates. It is ensuring that experimental results—including failures and uncertain outcomes—return to the modelling workflow in a structured, usable form.

Central questionHow can laboratory data be fed back into AI systems so that each round of discovery makes the next one more informed?

02From prediction to learning: how the feedback loop works

A prediction is a model’s estimate of what may happen when an untested molecule, protein, or experimental condition is evaluated. Experimental validation tests that estimate under defined conditions. Model updating is the separate step in which the measured result is incorporated into the data used to refine the model or guide future experiments.

This distinction separates open-loop from closed-loop discovery. In an open-loop workflow, a model proposes candidates and a laboratory tests them, but the results are not systematically connected back to the modelling process. In a closed-loop workflow, experimental outcomes inform the next round of predictions and candidate selection.

Bayesian optimization provides one way to implement this cycle. A surrogate model learns from accumulated observations, while an acquisition function helps select the next experiment based on its predicted potential and/or information value. Self-driving laboratories extend this approach by integrating automated experiments, characterization, and computational decision-making [5][12].

However, predictions and measurements can disagree for reasons beyond model error. A model may extrapolate beyond its training data, the assay may measure a different property from the one predicted, or technical variation may distort the result. In biological experiments, batch effects associated with laboratory conditions, reagent lots, or personnel can confound measurements, particularly when these effects correlate with the outcome of interest [3].

Consequently, an experimental result should be more than a number in a spreadsheet. A useful record connects the original design to its measured outcome and includes relevant assay conditions, controls, replicates, quality-control results, and uncertainty. Assay interference can also generate misleading signals in drug-discovery screens, making validation and careful interpretation essential [4].

Failed experiments can be informative, too. A protein that fails to express is not equivalent to one that expresses successfully but shows no activity. Recording these outcomes separately helps preserve what the experiment actually established.

The principle is simple: feedback can improve model-guided discovery, but only when measurements are reliable, interpretable, and connected to the decisions they are meant to inform.

03How the AI–laboratory feedback loop works

A closed-loop AI–laboratory workflow is not defined by robots or a particular algorithm. It is defined by a repeated process: generate candidates, test them, return structured results, update the model or decision strategy, and select the next experiments.

  1. Candidate generation and prediction. The model proposes candidate molecules, proteins, or experimental conditions and estimates their likely outcomes. Ideally, it also reports uncertainty, helping researchers distinguish confident predictions from less certain ones.
  2. Experiment selection. Researchers choose which candidates to test, considering predicted performance, uncertainty, experimental cost, available materials, and feasibility. Active learning and Bayesian optimization can guide this choice, but expert judgment and simpler selection rules can also be used.
  3. Laboratory execution and measurement. Selected candidates are synthesized, expressed, or otherwise prepared and tested. Experiments may be manual, semi-automated, or robotic. The laboratory records the measured outcomes alongside relevant experimental details.
  4. Sample identity tracking and quality control. Each candidate needs a stable identifier connecting its original design to the physical sample, raw instrument data, and final measurement. Quality control then identifies unreliable measurements, failed builds, and possible technical artefacts. Without this linkage, experimental results can be assigned to the wrong design or become unusable for model updating.
  5. Data integration and model updating. The processed measurement is linked to its original prediction and experimental context. The model or decision strategy can then be updated through retraining, recalibration, or revised candidate-selection rules. A polymer self-driving-laboratory study illustrates why improvement is not guaranteed: its reported model (R²) increased from 0.889 on the initial dataset to 0.946 after the first feedback generation, but declined in later generations as the selected formulations became more complex [8].
  6. Evaluation and next-experiment selection. The updated system selects the next batch of experiments. Researchers assess whether the workflow is improving prediction quality, finding better candidates, or using fewer experiments to reach a target. The cycle then repeats.

The essential requirement is traceable, interpretable feedback. Automation, generative AI, active learning, and full model retraining can strengthen the workflow, but none is individually required for a feedback loop to operate. Related self-driving-laboratory work: [16]; catalyst study: [1].

04Why data quality determines whether the loop works

Experimental feedback is useful only when the model can answer three questions about every result: Which candidate was tested? What was measured? Under what conditions?

Traceability begins with stable identifiers linking the original candidate to its physical sample, raw data files, and processed measurement. The FAIR data principles emphasize persistent identifiers, rich metadata, and provenance so that data can be found, interpreted, and reused [13]. Without this link, a measurement may exist, but the model cannot reliably connect it to the prediction it was meant to test.

Metadata provides scientific context. A result such as “42% inhibition” is incomplete without information about the assay, target, concentration, experimental conditions, controls, replicates, and processing method. The MIAME guidelines for microarray experiments illustrate this principle by requiring sample annotations, experimental design, raw and processed data, and the protocols used to generate them [14].

Failures must be recorded accurately. A construct that fails to express is not equivalent to one that expresses successfully but shows no biological activity. Similarly, a failed quality-control check is different from a genuine negative result. These outcomes should be classified separately so that the model does not learn the wrong lesson from an experiment.

Laboratory variation also matters. Differences in reagent lots, instruments, personnel, and experimental batches can introduce systematic effects that resemble biological signals. Assay interference can likewise produce misleading results through mechanisms such as aggregation, chemical reactivity, chelation, and optical interference [3][4]. Without suitable controls and contextual metadata, a model may learn technical artefacts rather than the property researchers intend to predict.

Finally, experiment-selection history matters. Active-learning systems deliberately choose which candidates to test, so researchers must document selection decisions and use appropriate evaluation procedures to avoid misleading estimates of model performance [5].

Practical principleMetadata does not make experimental data perfect; it makes the data interpretable, traceable, and auditable. Reliable feedback requires a clear connection between the design, the experiment, the result, and the conditions under which that result was obtained.

05How experimental feedback changes the model

When laboratory results return, they can improve an AI-driven discovery workflow in several different ways. The distinction matters because updating a model, choosing better experiments, and improving predictive accuracy are related—but not identical—outcomes.

  1. Retraining on accumulated data. New measurements can be combined with previous observations to retrain a model, which can learn from a larger dataset and revise its predictions. However, retraining can overfit recent batches if the evaluation process is poorly designed.
  2. Fine-tuning or incremental updating. Instead of rebuilding a model, researchers can adjust an existing model using new observations. This may reduce computational costs, but it can also cause older patterns to be forgotten or make predictions less reliable if recent data are unrepresentative.
  3. Active learning and experiment selection. Sometimes the most important change is not to the model itself, but to the experiments it chooses. Active learning uses current predictions and, where available, uncertainty estimates to prioritize informative candidates. This can help researchers discover useful compounds with fewer experiments, although its effectiveness depends on the model, data quality, and selection strategy [19].
  4. Uncertainty calibration and ranking. A model should not only estimate an outcome; researchers also need to understand how much to trust that estimate. Calibration assesses whether predicted confidence corresponds to observed performance. Updating a ranking or Bayesian-optimization surrogate may be sufficient when the immediate goal is to choose the next experimental batch rather than improve every prediction.

Better predictions do not always mean better experiments

Two outcomes must be evaluated separately:

  • Predictive performance: Does the model make more accurate predictions on data it has not trained on? Suitable metrics include mean absolute error, ranking performance, and calibration.
  • Campaign efficiency: Does the workflow find better candidates using fewer experiments, less time, or less material?

A model might become more accurate overall without improving the discovery campaign if its remaining errors occur among the candidates that matter most. Conversely, a model with little change in overall accuracy might still improve experimental efficiency by ranking promising candidates more effectively.

The strongest evidence comes from evaluating predictions on held-out data and comparing experimental outcomes against a defined baseline under the same budget. Retrospective simulations can support a hypothesis about effectiveness, but do not alone establish prospective laboratory benefit. Experimental feedback creates an opportunity to improve the model or the next decision. Whether it actually does so must be measured [1][5].

06Evidence from real-world discovery campaigns

Closed-loop learning has been demonstrated across drug discovery, protein engineering, materials science, and chemical catalysis. These studies show that experimental measurements can change subsequent model predictions or experiment selection—but they also reveal that improvement is not always consistent across rounds.

Protein engineering: learning from experimental outcomes

Yang et al. (2025) used active learning-assisted directed evolution to improve a protoglobin enzyme for a cyclopropanation reaction. After an initial variant library was tested, measured reaction yields and product selectivity were returned to the model to guide two further experimental rounds. The best variant reached 99% total yield and a 14:1 cis product ratio, compared with approximately 40% yield and a 3:1 trans preference for the parent enzyme. This demonstrates a functioning experimental feedback loop with a measurable improvement in the engineering objective. However, the study’s broader comparisons against alternative directed-evolution strategies relied partly on simulations, and the wet-lab results concern one enzyme–reaction system [6]. Note: the paper’s abstract summarises the wet-lab results differently, reporting desired-product yield rising from 12% to 93% over three wet-lab rounds.

Drug discovery: feedback can change the search strategy

Reker et al. (2016) used active machine learning to identify small-molecule inhibitors of the CXCR4–CXCL12 protein–protein interaction. Experimental inhibition measurements informed subsequent model updates and compound selection. The campaign included both exploratory and more exploitation-focused rounds. The outcomes were not uniformly successful: the second screening round produced weak or no activity, while six of ten compounds in the third round showed strong readouts. The study demonstrates prospective closed-loop drug discovery, but it does not establish a clean, controlled reduction in predictive error across rounds [7].

Materials discovery: feedback can help—and later become less effective

Suponya et al. (2026) developed a self-driving laboratory for designing single-chain polymer nanoparticles. After an initial set of formulations, the system repeatedly selected new candidates, synthesized them, measured their hydrodynamic radius, and updated its Gaussian-process model. After the first feedback generation, the mean hydrodynamic radius decreased from 6.6 nm to 3.6 nm, while the model’s R² increased from 0.889 to 0.946. In later generations, R² declined to 0.905 and the mean radius increased to 4.3 nm. The authors discuss limitations including measurement artefacts and the increasing complexity of selected formulations [8]. This is an important counterexample to the assumption that every feedback cycle improves performance.

Catalyst discovery: a clear reduction in prediction error

Wang et al. (2023) used Bayesian optimization to discover stereoselective aluminum catalysts. Measured outcomes were added to the dataset after each of three rounds, refining the model’s subsequent predictions. The mean absolute prediction error fell from 0.36 in round one to 0.10 in round three, while later rounds identified more high-performing catalysts [1]. Although this study concerns polymer chemistry rather than biology, it provides a particularly clear quantitative example of prediction, measurement, model updating, and improved subsequent predictions.

What the evidence establishesTogether, these studies demonstrate that experimental feedback can guide future decisions and improve specific discovery outcomes. They do not establish that feedback invariably improves model accuracy or that the same strategy will work equally well across all biological systems. The relevant question is whether the returned measurements improve the predictions, candidate rankings, or experimental efficiency that matter for the intended task.

08Why closing the loop does not guarantee improvement

Closed-loop machine learning can make experimental discovery more efficient, but it does not eliminate the challenges of modelling biology. The value of feedback depends on the reliability of measurements, the representativeness of the data, and how the model uses the returned results.

Noisy measurements can mislead the model. Assay variability, instrument drift, and inconsistent experimental conditions can produce unreliable labels. This is especially problematic in active learning, where uncertainty-based selection may prioritize examples that are noisy rather than genuinely informative. Research has shown that active learning can struggle when label noise varies across the data distribution [17][18].

Batch effects can masquerade as biology. Differences in laboratory conditions, reagent lots, instruments, and personnel can introduce systematic variation. If these factors correlate with the outcome, a model may learn technical artefacts instead of the biological relationship researchers want to predict. Replication, controls, randomization, and recording relevant experimental covariates help reduce this risk [3].

Models may not generalize across contexts. A model trained using one cell type, laboratory, assay, or experimental condition may perform poorly in another. This distribution shift means that success on familiar data does not guarantee reliable predictions in a new biological setting. Independent validation across relevant contexts is therefore essential.

Biased selection can limit what the model learns. If researchers test only candidates already predicted to succeed, the model receives limited information about other regions of the design space. Similarly, a failed build, a quality-control failure, and a genuinely inactive candidate should not automatically be treated as equivalent negative outcomes. Recording selection decisions and distinct failure categories helps preserve the information needed for future learning.

Data leakage can create misleading improvements. If information from test data enters training, or closely related samples appear in both training and evaluation sets, measured performance may be overly optimistic. Biological datasets require careful splitting strategies, especially when the intended application involves new sequences, laboratories, or conditions [9].

Finally, more data do not automatically mean a better model. New measurements may be redundant, noisy, unrepresentative, or poorly matched to the discovery objective. Feedback can improve predictions or experimental efficiency, but those improvements must be demonstrated against suitable evaluation data and baselines.

The defensible conclusion is not that every feedback cycle makes a model better. It is that reliable, well-documented, informative experimental feedback creates an opportunity for improvement—provided that the resulting changes are rigorously evaluated.

09Closing perspective: predictions become useful when experiments answer back

A model’s prediction is a hypothesis about what may happen, not evidence that it has happened. An experiment tests that hypothesis; a feedback loop begins to deliver value when the resulting measurement is linked to the correct candidate, interpreted in its experimental context, and used to inform subsequent decisions.

The distinction matters. Collecting more laboratory data does not automatically improve a model. Measurements can be noisy, experimental conditions can introduce batch effects, and a model can appear to improve without generalizing to genuinely new candidates or settings. Reliable feedback therefore requires traceable sample and candidate identities, relevant assay metadata, documented quality control, and clear distinctions between true negative results, technical failures, and missing measurements. Batch effects and information leakage are established threats to reliable biological-data analysis and model evaluation [3][9].

The model must then use the returned data appropriately—whether through retraining, targeted updating, or improved experiment selection—and its performance must be assessed against suitable baselines and independent evaluation data. The objective is not to make every cycle look successful, but to learn which predictions are reliable, where uncertainty remains, and which experiment is most informative next.

The practical implication for AI-driven experimental biology and drug discovery is clear: laboratories and modelling teams should design their data systems together, so each experimental result can be traced, quality-checked, interpreted in context, and evaluated before it influences the next round of decisions.
